# Authentic Movement

## Overview

Authentic Movement is a contemplative movement practice that explores the relationship between the inner witness and the moving self. In this profound yet simple practice, a mover closes their eyes and follows inner impulses, sensations, and images, while a witness observes with compassionate, non-judgmental attention. After moving, both mover and witness speak about their experiences, bringing consciousness to what has emerged from the depths. Through this process, practitioners develop embodied consciousness, symbolic awareness, and the capacity for compassionate witnessing of self and others.

The practice bridges movement and depth psychology, somatics and meditation, individual process and relational encounter. It recognizes that the body carries wisdom beyond cognitive knowing - that when we allow movement to arise from inner sources rather than external choreography, we access unconscious material, archetypal images, and deep personal truth. The practice provides a container for this material to emerge, be witnessed, and be integrated through conscious reflection.

What makes Authentic Movement distinctive is its emphasis on witnessing consciousness - both external (being witnessed by another) and internal (developing the capacity to witness oneself). The practice cultivates what is sometimes called "the inner witness" - a capacity for compassionate self-observation that is neither judgmental nor dissociated but lovingly present to all experience. This witnessing consciousness, developed through repeated practice, becomes a resource for living more consciously and compassionately.

## Historical Context and Development

### Mary Starks Whitehouse: Movement-in-Depth

Mary Starks Whitehouse (1911-1979) is considered the mother of Authentic Movement, though she didn't use that term. A dancer and dance therapist trained in Jungian psychology, Whitehouse developed what she called "movement-in-depth" in the 1950s and 1960s, working at the Jung Institute in Los Angeles and in private practice.

Whitehouse was trained in modern dance and Graham technique but became increasingly interested in improvisation and the spontaneous emergence of movement. Influenced by her Jungian analysis and study of active imagination, she began exploring movement as a way to access and work with unconscious material.

#### Key Concepts from Whitehouse:

**"I move" vs. "I am moved":**
Whitehouse distinguished between movement that comes from conscious intention and will ("I move") and movement that arises from a deeper source, often experienced as happening to the person rather than being consciously chosen ("I am moved"). She was interested in the latter - movements that surprise the mover, that have symbolic meaning, that come from the unconscious.

**Active Imagination in Movement:**
Drawing on Jung's practice of active imagination (dialoguing with figures from dreams and fantasies), Whitehouse used movement as a way to embody and give form to inner images and impulses. The body becomes the site where unconscious material manifests and can be worked with directly.

**The Witness:**
Whitehouse recognized the importance of being witnessed - having another person present, attentively observing without judgment. She also emphasized developing the capacity to witness oneself - to observe one's experience while still engaged in it.

### Janet Adler: Authentic Movement as Discipline

Janet Adler, who studied with Whitehouse, developed Authentic Movement as a distinct discipline of practice in the 1970s and 1980s. Where Whitehouse worked primarily in one-to-one therapeutic contexts, Adler created structures for group practice and articulated a comprehensive philosophy of the practice.

Adler emphasized Authentic Movement as a meditation practice and spiritual discipline, not just a therapeutic technique. She developed:

**The Circle:**
Group structure where multiple movers move simultaneously in a circle while one or more witnesses observe. The circle creates collective container and community of practice.

**Three Levels of Practice:**
1. **Personal process** - Working with one's own material
2. **Interpersonal process** - The relationship between mover and witness
3. **Collective process** - The field created by the group, transpersonal dimensions

**Speaking from Experience:**
The practice of speaking after moving, saying "I" statements about direct experience rather than interpreting or analyzing. This phenomenological approach keeps practice grounded in actual experience.

**The Consciousness of the Witness:**
Adler's later work focused increasingly on witness consciousness itself - the quality of attention that can hold all experience with compassion. She taught that developing this witness consciousness is the deepest purpose of the practice.

### Joan Chodorow: Integration with Jungian Psychology

Joan Chodorow, a dance therapist and Jungian analyst, articulated connections between Authentic Movement and Jung's analytical psychology. Her book *Dance Therapy and Depth Psychology* (1991) provided theoretical framework connecting movement practice with Jungian concepts: active imagination, archetypes, individuation, and the transcendent function.

Chodorow emphasized how movement work accesses the symbolic dimension - that movements and gestures carry meanings beyond their literal form, connecting personal experience to archetypal patterns.

### Contemporary Developers

**Tina Stromsted:**
- Integrated Authentic Movement with somatic psychology and trauma work
- Developed understanding of how practice supports healing from trauma
- Emphasized somatic resources and regulation

**Aileen Crow:**
- Focus on mystical and spiritual dimensions
- Emphasis on collective body and transpersonal experience
- Teaching internationally

**Saphira Linden:**
- Writing and teaching on witness consciousness
- Integration with Buddhist meditation practices
- Applications to social healing and collective trauma

**Samuel Avstreih:**
- Philosopher and practitioner
- Wrote *The Lived Body* exploring phenomenology of the practice

Many other practitioners have developed the work in various directions while maintaining core principles established by Whitehouse and Adler.

## Core Principles and Structures

### The Basic Form

At its simplest, Authentic Movement involves:

**The Mover:**
- Closes eyes
- Waits for impulse to move
- Follows inner sensations, images, impulses
- Moves in response to what arises internally
- Stays present to experience

**The Witness:**
- Sits in designated place
- Observes the mover with full, compassionate attention
- Does not interpret or judge
- Holds space
- Later speaks from their experience of witnessing

**The Container:**
- Defined space (often a circle)
- Agreed-upon time frame
- Safety and confidentiality
- Structure holds the practice

**Speaking:**
- After moving, both mover and witness speak
- Mover speaks first (usually)
- Speaking from "I" statements about direct experience
- No interpretation or advice
- Simple, phenomenological description

This basic structure can be practiced one-to-one, in pairs taking turns, or in groups where multiple movers move while one or more witnesses observe.

### Waiting for the Impulse

A key discipline is waiting - not immediately moving but staying present in stillness until a genuine inner impulse arises. This waiting cultivates:
- Tolerance for not-knowing
- Distinction between willful action and authentic impulse
- Trust in the body's wisdom
- Capacity to listen internally

Many movers discover that their most meaningful movements come after waiting through initial restlessness or anxiety.

### Following vs. Leading

Authentic Movement practice emphasizes following what arises rather than leading from conscious intention. This requires:
- Surrender of control
- Trust in the process
- Tolerance for vulnerability
- Willingness to be surprised by what emerges

The mover learns to track inner experience and move in response to it rather than imposing predetermined ideas about what should happen.

### Eyes Closed

Moving with eyes closed:
- Shifts attention inward
- Reduces self-consciousness and performing
- Diminishes visual dominance, activating other senses
- Creates conditions for symbolic and imaginal experience to emerge
- Requires trust (in witness, in space, in process)

The eyes-closed practice is central but not absolute; experienced movers sometimes move with eyes open or partially open, depending on what's arising.

### The Witness's Attention

The witness's quality of attention is crucial:
- **Present** - Fully here, not distracted
- **Non-judgmental** - No criticism, evaluation, or interpretation
- **Compassionate** - Holding the mover with care
- **Spacious** - Allowing whatever arises
- **Embodied** - Witness tracks their own bodily experience while observing

The witness is not passive but actively present, offering conscious attention as gift and container.

### Inner Witness

A fundamental goal is developing "inner witness" - the capacity to observe one's own experience with the same compassionate, non-judgmental attention. This inner witness:
- Provides internal safety and support
- Enables staying present with difficult experience
- Facilitates integration of unconscious material
- Supports conscious living beyond formal practice

The external witness eventually internalizes, though relationship with external witness remains valuable.

### Speaking from Experience

After moving, the practice of speaking serves multiple functions:
- Brings consciousness to what was non-verbal
- Allows integration through articulation
- Provides witness with understanding of mover's experience
- Develops capacity to stay with phenomenological description
- Reveals patterns over time

The discipline is speaking from direct experience: "I noticed...", "I felt...", "An image appeared..." rather than interpretation or analysis.

## Psychological and Spiritual Dimensions

### Working with the Unconscious

Authentic Movement provides a way to access unconscious material through embodiment. Rather than working with dreams or fantasies purely cognitively, the practice allows unconscious content to manifest in movement, making it more tangible and workable.

Material that emerges might include:
- Forgotten or traumatic memories
- Archetypal images and energies
- Shadow material
- Deep desires or fears
- Developmental patterns from childhood
- Transpersonal or spiritual experiences

### Symbolic and Archetypal Dimensions

Movements often carry symbolic meaning. A mover might:
- Curl into fetal position (birth/death imagery)
- Make pushing gestures (boundaries, resistance)
- Reach upward repeatedly (aspiration, prayer)
- Move in spiral patterns (transformation, cycles)
- Embody archetypal figures (warrior, mother, crone)

These symbols connect personal experience to universal human patterns.

### The Transcendent Function

Jung's concept of the transcendent function - the psyche's capacity to hold and integrate opposites, creating something new - manifests in Authentic Movement. The practice creates conditions where:
- Conscious and unconscious meet
- Body and psyche integrate
- Opposites can be held (stillness and movement, control and surrender)
- New possibilities emerge

### Meditation and Witness Consciousness

The practice is fundamentally meditative. Cultivation of witness consciousness - the capacity to observe experience without identification or judgment - parallels Buddhist meditation practices of mindfulness and equanimity. Some practitioners understand Authentic Movement as moving meditation.

### Relationship and the Between

The relationship between mover and witness creates a "third" - the field between them where healing and development occur. This relational dimension:
- Provides experiences of being seen and held
- Heals early attachment wounds
- Develops capacity for authentic relationship
- Creates sacred space

### Developmental and Trauma-Informed Understanding

Contemporary practice integrates understanding of:
- Attachment patterns manifesting in movement
- Trauma held in the body
- Need for safety, resources, titration when working with difficult material
- Developmental stages and needs
- Somatic experiencing and regulation

This integration makes the practice safer and more therapeutically effective, especially for people with trauma histories.

## Practical Application and Experience

### In Individual Sessions

Working one-to-one with a trained practitioner:
- Session typically 60-90 minutes
- May include check-in discussion
- 20-30 minutes of moving
- Speaking about the experience
- Dialogue and integration
- Used therapeutically, as spiritual practice, or for artistic development

### In Group Practice

Groups might meet:
- Weekly ongoing groups (building depth through continuity)
- Workshops (intensive periods of practice)
- Retreats (extended immersion)

Group structures vary:
- **One-on-one within circle** - Pairs working while others witness
- **Multiple movers, one witness** - Several moving simultaneously
- **Collective witnessing** - All taking responsibility for holding space
- **Large group moving** - All moving together in shared field

### Training and Deepening

Many practitioners:
- Engage in regular ongoing practice
- Participate in training programs (if becoming teachers)
- Maintain relationship with teacher/mentor
- Integrate practice with therapy, spiritual practice, creative work
- Move through developmental stages in the practice

### Speaking and Language

Learning to speak from experience is its own discipline:
- Moving from interpretation to description
- Staying with "I" statements
- Honoring silence and not-knowing
- Developing capacity for precise language about subtle experience
- Learning when speaking serves and when it deflects

### Therapeutic Applications

Used in:
- Depth psychology and Jungian analysis
- Trauma healing and PTSD treatment
- Grief and loss work
- Addiction recovery
- Eating disorder treatment
- Body image and embodiment issues
- Relationship therapy

### Creative Development

Artists use the practice for:
- Accessing creative source material
- Breaking through blocks
- Developing choreography and performance
- Deepening artistic authenticity
- Exploring images and themes

### Spiritual Practice

As contemplative practice:
- Developing meditation and presence
- Working with prayer and devotion through movement
- Accessing mystical experiences
- Integrating body and spirit
- Community and sangha

### Personal Development

General benefits:
- Increased body awareness and comfort
- Emotional fluency and regulation
- Self-knowledge and insight
- Capacity for authentic relationship
- Development of witness consciousness
- Integration of various aspects of self

## Training and Finding Practice

### Becoming a Teacher

No single certifying body exists; various teachers and programs offer training:
- Adler's lineage and associated teachers
- Chodorow's teaching
- Various contemporary teachers' programs
- Usually involves:
  - Extensive personal practice (years)
  - Formal training (often 3+ years)
  - Supervision and mentorship
  - Integration with psychology, somatics, or related field
  - Ongoing personal therapy/analysis

### Finding Teachers and Groups

**Authentic Movement Community:**
- Loose international network
- Teacher/practitioner directories (various websites)
- Word of mouth within somatic and depth psychology communities

**Related Organizations:**
- American Dance Therapy Association (some members teach AM)
- Various Jungian and depth psychology institutes
- Somatic psychology programs

**Formats:**
- Ongoing practice groups (best for depth)
- Workshops and intensives
- Individual sessions with trained practitioners
- Training programs

### Starting Practice

For newcomers:
- Find experienced teacher (training and depth matter)
- Attend intro workshop before committing
- Consider individual sessions to learn basics
- Read foundational texts to understand principles
- Be patient - the practice deepens over time

### Essential Reading

**Foundational Texts:**
- *Authentic Movement: Moving the Body, Moving the Self, Being Moved* edited by Patrizia Pallaro (1999) - Collection of essays by major teachers, comprehensive introduction
- *Authentic Movement: Essays by Mary Starks Whitehouse, Janet Adler and Joan Chodorow* edited by Patrizia Pallaro (1999) - Primary writings by founders
- *Offering from the Conscious Body: The Discipline of Authentic Movement* by Janet Adler (2002) - Adler's mature articulation of the practice
- *Dance Therapy and Depth Psychology: The Moving Imagination* by Joan Chodorow (1991) - Integration with Jungian psychology

**Contemporary Works:**
- *The Lived Body: A Phenomenological Essay on the Mortal Condition* by Samuel Avstreih (1999) - Philosophical exploration

## Challenges and Considerations

### Depth and Time

Authentic Movement reveals its depths slowly:
- Benefits accrue through sustained practice
- Quick results or dramatic experiences aren't the goal
- Requires patience and commitment

### Finding Quality Teaching

Teacher training and skill vary:
- Seek teachers with substantial training and experience
- Personal resonance matters
- Check references and lineage
- Be wary of weekend training courses claiming to certify teachers

### Not Appropriate for Everyone

The practice may not suit:
- Those currently in acute crisis
- Some trauma survivors (without appropriate support)
- People seeking structured movement or exercise
- Those uncomfortable with eyes-closed, inward focus

Working with qualified practitioners who screen appropriately and provide adequate support is essential.

### Vulnerability and Safety

The practice can access deep material:
- Requires safe container
- Needs skilled, ethical practitioners
- Important to have appropriate support (therapy, etc.)
- Not a substitute for needed mental health treatment

## Conclusion

Authentic Movement offers a unique path to consciousness through the body. Its genius lies in the simple yet profound structure: moving with eyes closed, being witnessed, speaking from experience. Through this practice, repeated over time, practitioners develop capacities central to human development: embodied awareness, symbolic consciousness, compassionate self-witnessing, and authentic relationship.

The practice honors the body as source of wisdom, recognizing that when we follow inner impulses rather than imposing conscious will, we access depths usually hidden. Material from the unconscious - images, memories, archetypal energies - can emerge and be worked with directly, not just intellectually but somatically, where it actually lives.

What makes Authentic Movement particularly valuable in the context of human potential is its integration of multiple dimensions: psychological and spiritual, individual and relational, somatic and symbolic. It provides methodology for ongoing development that doesn't end with symptom relief or particular achievement but continues to deepen throughout life.

The development of witness consciousness may be the practice's most profound gift. In a world that constantly pulls attention outward and judges harshly, cultivating compassionate self-witnessing - the capacity to observe one's experience with kindness and curiosity rather than judgment - supports not just personal well-being but more conscious, compassionate living in the world.

Mary Whitehouse's question - "What is the difference between I move and I am moved?" - remains central. Authentic Movement invites us into relationship with depths beyond ego-control, trusting that the body knows something the conscious mind doesn't, that movement arising from inner sources carries meaning and supports development. This trust - in the body, in the unconscious, in the process - represents a fundamental shift from the culturally dominant stance of control and manipulation toward one of listening, following, and serving something larger than the individual ego.

For those drawn to contemplative practice, depth psychology, and embodied spirituality, Authentic Movement offers a profound discipline. It requires no particular movement skill, just willingness to close the eyes, wait for impulse, follow what arises, and speak truthfully about the experience. Through this simple practice, sustained over time in relationship with skillful teachers and committed fellow practitioners, possibilities for consciousness, compassion, and authentic being continue to unfold.
